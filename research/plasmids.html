Plasmid replication control

Plasmids are small circles of DNA that copy themselves inside bacteria, independently of the chromosome. To survive, they must keep their copy number in check: too few and they get lost when the cell divides, too many and they become a burden to their host.

The question

How do plasmids sense how many copies of themselves are in a cell, and how precisely can they correct deviations? Answering this means following replication control in individual cells over many generations, rather than averaging over large populations.

Approach

In the lab of Johan Paulsson at Harvard Medical School, I combine advanced microscopy with microfluidics to follow plasmid replication in single cells, and use quantitative analysis to test models of how that control works.

Counting plasmids one copy at a time

Plasmid copy numbers have mostly been measured in bulk, which gives the average over millions of cells but hides how much individual cells differ. Yet those cell-to-cell fluctuations are exactly what the control system is selected to suppress. I use high- and super-resolution imaging to count individual copies of the mini-R1 plasmid in single cells, resolving where the copies sit and how their number changes across the cell cycle and over many generations. Combining this with high-throughput microfluidics, which follows very large numbers of cells at once, lets us catch rare and extreme deviations, such as cells that lose the plasmid altogether. Together, these measurements make it possible to test how robust control emerges from noisy molecular processes.
